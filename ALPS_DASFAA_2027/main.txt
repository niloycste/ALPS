     ALP S: Admissible Locality and Payload
    Semantics for Region-Based Parallel Mining

                               Anonymous Author(s)

                               Anonymous Institution


      Abstract. Semantic transaction partitioning is not only an optimisation
      problem; the partition must also work correctly under the execution
      model. In region-based parallel mining, all regions validate transactions
      concurrently from the same start-of-round state, so payload clustering
      can place operations on the same state in different regions. Within this
      independent-barrier additive-merge model with no coordination, the pa-
      per characterises guarded-key locality as necessary and sufficient for
      balance non-negativity; it makes no completeness, liveness, or general
      EVM-validity claim. Ethereum’s global account nonce creates cross-region
      ordering dependencies when a sender is split; ALPS enforces sender local-
      ity for the evaluated direct calls. The static allocator optimises payload
      similarity, conditional active-account interaction cut, and historical al-
      location load under a hard effective load constraint. Its fixed-horizon
      chronological replay freezes feature scaling from an earlier prefix and has
      no lifetime load-cap guarantee. On a 34,326-call Ethereum trace with six
      regions, transaction k-means splits 1,021 guarded keys and takes 1,068
      barrier rounds, while ALPS has zero split senders and guarded keys and
      takes 27 rounds. Across three Ethereum mainnet traces and one BNB
      Smart Chain trace (145,372 calls), ALPS improves retrieval locality and
      conditional interaction cut over address hashing in all 24 trace/region
      settings. Compared with balanced sender k-means, ALPS reduces the
      cut in all 24 settings by 12.8–82.5%; R90 improves in 19 settings, ties in
      two, and trades off in three. The contribution is a scoped balance-safety
      formalisation and a cost-selectable allocator, not universal sharding safety
      or production TPS.

state conflicts, Ethereum


1   Introduction
State-based blockchains usually process transactions in sequence because trans-
action validity depends on a shared and changing state [?]. Region-based parallel
mining [?] reduces this bottleneck by dividing pending transactions among re-
gions. Each region mines independently, and the regional blocks are joined at a
barrier into a super-block. The original proposal assigns the initiating account
by address modulo but permits configurable assignment. This work targets its
transaction-allocation and scheduling layer, not consensus mining performance.
2         Anonymous Author(s)

    This flexibility creates an opportunity to improve transaction locality. Recent
work has explored semantic sharding for blockchain retrieval [?] and workload-
aware account placement to reduce cross-shard traffic or adapt to changing
workloads [?,?,?]. Conflict-oriented systems instead change cross-shard ordering or
scheduling to avoid conflicting execution [?, ?]. These directions motivate smarter
placement, but they do not characterise admissibility for the independent-barrier
model considered here. Transactions can be grouped by application payload so
that semantically related records stay in the same region, reducing the number of
regions scanned by an asset query. However, payload describes an operation, not
the state that the operation conditionally consumes. Two miners can therefore
validate debits of the same balance against the same stale snapshot. Guarded-key
locality protects balances; stronger sender locality also keeps Ethereum’s account-
wide nonce dependencies region-local. Therefore, high clustering quality does not
guarantee an executable blockchain schedule, while simple address hashing loses
useful locality. This paper asks what state-locality invariant is required by the
independent-barrier model and how much semantic and interaction locality can
be recovered while enforcing it as a hard constraint. The work follows three steps:
characterise model-specific admissibility, choose an allocation unit that preserves
it, and optimise multiple locality objectives only within that feasible space.
    The main contributions of this paper are:

    – A necessary-and-sufficient characterisation of balance-safety admissibility for
      concurrent, same-snapshot regional execution with additive merging and no
      cross-region serialization, formalising a single-region guarded-key discipline.
    – ALPS (Admissible Locality and Payload Semantics), a sender-indivisible
      allocator that combines weighted semantic prototypes, an interaction-graph
      cut, and a load penalty under a hard static historical-load constraint. Exact
      whole-sender moves give monotone local refinement; the related chronological
      heuristic pins senders but has no lifetime load-cap guarantee.
    – A balance-safety and locality evaluation over four direct-call traces (three
      Ethereum and one BNB Smart Chain; 145,372 calls) and a separate 17,445-
      event log trace. It uses observed signer-nonce order, synthetic per-token
      balance stress, static tests, fixed-horizon replays, ablations, and a reimple-
      mented TxAllo allocation-objective comparison; locality gains and load/cut
      trade-offs are reported.

    The scope is limited to the independent-barrier model. Protocols with locks,
optimistic validation, cross-shard atomicity, or re-execution can safely use more
general partitions, but they require a coordination mechanism that the model
studied here omits.
    The rest of this paper is organised as follows. Section II reviews related
work. Section III presents the proposed work, including the execution model,
admissibility conditions, and the proposed ALPS method. Section IV describes
the experimental setup. Section V presents the performance evaluation. Section VI
concludes the paper and outlines future work.

2   Related Work

Prior work can be grouped into conflict-aware parallel execution, coordinated
sharding, semantic sharding and retrieval, workload-aware placement, and database
partitioning. This grouping separates conflict-handling mechanisms from place-
ment objectives and places ALPS within the uncoordinated barrier model.
    Chishti and Banerjee [?] introduced the independent regional model studied
in this paper. Other approaches alter execution to handle conflicts explicitly.
Dickerson et al. [?] and Block-STM [?] detect and re-execute conflicts, while static
analysis characterises parallelism [?]. ParBlockchain [?] and PEEP [?] encode
dependency schedules, Vegeta speculates and replays [?], and Sui Lutris supports
object-level independence [?]. Prophet uses Byzantine-tolerant deterministic
ordering to avoid conflicting cross-shard execution [?], while the recent Concordia
system targets low-conflict distributed transaction scheduling across shards [?].
These mechanisms or programming models are not part of the independent
barrier.
    Coordinated sharding also changes the execution protocol. SharPer coordinates
cross-shard transactions [?], while Monoxide provides eventual atomicity across
zones [?]. Therefore, the admissibility condition derived in Section ?? does not
restrict these systems; it characterises the cheaper uncoordinated barrier model.
    Semantic sharding has separately targeted retrieval and storage locality. Se-
mantiChain allocates transactions using semantic features and semantic distance,
and couples that placement with a distributed semantic retrieval architecture [?].
ALPS uses payload semantics for a different purpose: semantic locality is opti-
mised only after the allocation unit has been restricted to preserve admissibility
under same-snapshot independent regional execution.
    Workload-aware placement is the closest algorithmic line of work. OptChain
reduces cross-shard traffic using an online graph [?]; Shard Scheduler places and
migrates objects [?]; BrokerChain combines partitioning with broker accounts [?];
and TxAllo jointly optimises traffic and workload balance [?]. More recent work
extends account allocation in complementary directions: Orbit uses pending
transactions to react to changing traffic before packaging [?], whereas Mosaic
moves allocation toward client-driven local optimisation using interaction and
workload information [?]. ALPS instead combines retrieval locality, active-account
interaction cuts, guarded-state admissibility, and a hard historical-load bound in
its static allocator. Section ?? directly compares the TxAllo allocation objective.
Since A-TxAllo already places new nodes chronologically, the temporal test in
this paper is used as robustness evidence rather than a precedence claim.
    Database partitioning provides a broader comparison. Schism balances a co-
access graph to reduce distributed transactions [?]. ALPS follows the same general
principle but moves only whole senders, making the schedule admissible without
abort or retry coordination. The evaluation also applies weighted multilevel
METIS [?] directly to the active-sender graph, separating a general graph cut
from ALPS’s explicit semantic term and effective load constraint.

3     Proposed Work

This section first defines the independent-barrier execution model and its ad-
missibility requirements. It then presents the proposed ALPS method for static
allocation and fixed-horizon chronological replay.


3.1   System Model and Admissibility

The model imposes a balance-safety locality condition and exposes a separate
nonce-ordering cost when senders are fragmented under independent regional
execution.


Independent-barrier Execution Let T be a transaction set and P : T →
{1, . . . , n} its static region assignment. At the start of round k, every region reads
the same committed state σk . Each region executes concurrently with the others,
processes its transactions sequentially on a private copy, and accepts a debit
only if its local balance is sufficient; an insufficient-balance debit is rejected in
that execution. Regions neither observe nor serialize with one another during the
round. At the barrier, all accepted debit and credit deltas are merged additively
to form σk+1 . This follows the execution structure of the base parallel-mining
design [?]; proof-of-work is independent of the state argument considered here.
     An ERC-20 balance is associated with both an account and a token contract.
Let b = (a, c) denote the balance of account a in token contract c. A transfer
t = (a, d, c, x, q) conditionally consumes x from debit key g(t) = (a, c), credits
(d, c), and carries account-wide nonce q. Credits are unconditional additions,
whereas debits are guarded non-commutative operations.
     For balance safety, all transactions that debit the same guarded key must
stay in the same region. This requirement, called guarded-key locality, can be
written as
                            g(ti ) = g(tj ) =⇒ P (ti ) = P (tj ).
A partition is balance-safe barrier-admissible if, for every non-negative starting
balance vector and every same-round transfer workload, independently validated
deltas cannot merge into a negative balance. This property concerns balance
non-negativity only, not nonce validity, completeness, or liveness.

Theorem 1 (Balance-Safety Admissibility). Under the concurrent same-
snapshot, additive-merge execution just defined, without cross-region serialization
or revalidation, a static transfer partition is balance-safe barrier-admissible for
every non-negative starting balance vector and same-round workload if and only
if it satisfies guarded-key locality.

Proof. For sufficiency, consider a guarded key b in round k. Guarded-key locality
implies that only one region r can debit b. Sequential local validation in that
region gives
                              Dr (b) ≤ σk (b) + Cr (b),

    For necessity, suppose the static assignment places two debits of the same
guarded key in different regions in the same round, with positive debit totals d1
and d2 , and no credits are made to that key. If the starting balance is max(d1 , d2 ),
both regions can accept their debits because they validate against the same
starting state. After the regional changes are merged, the balance becomes
                             max(d1 , d2 ) − d1 − d2 < 0.
Hence any such partition fails to be balance-safe barrier-admissible for some
starting balance and same-round workload.
    This result is specific to the independent-barrier model. For example, Monoxide
provides asynchronous cross-zone transaction semantics [?], while Block-STM
detects conflicts and re-executes transactions [?]. These systems therefore do not
follow the uncoordinated merge assumed here. For the same direct ERC-20 balance
abstraction in a fuller Ethereum execution, nonce serialization, revalidation, or
scheduling may prevent simultaneous acceptance; guarded-key locality remains
sufficient, but this theorem does not claim it is necessary for those coordinated
models.

Sender Locality for Global Nonces Ethereum’s transaction nonce is keyed by
account rather than by token. Under the independent-barrier model, splitting one
sender across regions creates cross-region ordering dependencies: future-nonce
transactions may need to wait for lower nonces committed elsewhere. ALPS
therefore enforces sender locality, placing all transactions from sender a, across
all tokens, in one region so that nonce order can progress without cross-barrier
deferral. Sender locality is stronger than guarded-key locality for the evaluated
direct calls, where the outer signer is the token debitor; it does not automatically
extend to routers or arbitrary contract read/write sets. It preserves both token-
balance locality and account order structurally. This distinction between sender
fragmentation and sender-local allocation is illustrated in Figure ??. For example,
two calls from one signer that debit different tokens have different guarded keys,
so assigning each key to a separate region satisfies the balance theorem. If the
calls have consecutive account nonces, however, the higher-nonce call still depends
on the lower-nonce call committing first. Guarded-key locality alone therefore
does not guarantee nonce-correct progress.
Corollary 1 (Direct-Call Locality). For the evaluated direct ERC-20 calls,
where the outer signer is the debited token holder, sender locality implies guarded-
key locality and makes every submitted account-nonce dependency region-local.

Proof. All guarded keys (a, c) belonging to sender a map to the sender’s single

    If a sender is split across regions, a correct mempool must not permanently
reject a future-nonce transaction. Instead, the transaction should be deferred
and retried after lower nonces commit. This preserves eventual correctness, but
different fragments of the same sender sequence can progress only across barriers,
which increases the scheduling cost. Section ?? evaluates this effect. A separate
balance-only execution is also used as a deliberate stress test of the guarded-key
locality condition; it is not presented as a replacement for Ethereum’s nonce rule.

Fixed-Horizon Chronological Placement A chronological allocator cannot
build a sender profile from transactions that have not yet occurred. The temporal
version therefore learns prototypes and assignments only from a chronological
prefix. A known sender keeps its assigned region. A sender absent from the history
is assigned exactly once, when its first future transaction arrives. The assignment
uses the transaction’s visible payload, the receiver’s region when the receiver is
already placed, and the current future-prefix load. The selected region is then
pinned.
    Algorithm ?? is a fixed-horizon chronological replay, not a fully online deploy-
ment experiment: it predefines Hf = |Tf | for each evaluation suffix. Conditional
on that horizon and the prefix-fitted frozen scaling in Section ??, each placement
uses only the prefix observed so far. Later transactions do not affect earlier sender
assignments. Here, Lr counts transactions observed so far and Cf = αHf /n is
the horizon target. When an unseen sender first appears, regions that can accept
that transaction without crossing Cf form the candidate set; if none remain, all
regions are considered. The load term then acts as a soft cost within that set.
This admission test applies only when a sender is first pinned: later activity from

Chronological Time Complexity With a hash-map assignment table, routing a
transaction from an already pinned sender takes O(1) expected time. A previously
unseen sender is placed only once; evaluating its semantic and load-aware cost
over all n regions costs O(nd). Therefore, for a future stream of F transactions
containing U previously unseen senders, chronological placement costs

                                   O(F + U nd),                                  (4)

with the more expensive O(nd) decision paid only at each sender’s first appearance.
The assignment map grows by O(U ) entries over the future stream.
    This temporal split is used as a robustness check rather than a methodological
novelty claim. A-TxAllo also uses a chronological 9:1 partition, carries the previous
allocation forward, and explicitly places new nodes using newly committed
transactions [?]. The evaluation in this paper instead uses four fixed history
horizons, pins each sender at its first appearance, and evaluates retrieval together
with interaction cut and load. For fairness, every fixed-horizon replay baseline
carries its history assignment forward and assigns each unseen sender once using
that baseline’s rule; the assignment is then pinned. The replay allocator is a
related heuristic rather than the static graph objective: its graph term is replaced
by one currently placed receiver indicator.

4.2   Schemes and Execution

The main static evaluation compares all methods except METIS and TXALLO.
ADDR-MOD uses a mod n, TX-KMEANS clusters individual transactions, and
the sender-based variants cluster mean features of active senders. S-KM-BAL
additionally uses the 1.15× historical-load target, while ALPS uses the joint
objective described in Section ??. Section ?? separately compares METIS and
TXALLO.
    The evaluation varies n ∈ {2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 11} with a regional block capacity of 250.
For the global nonce experiment, each (account, token) debit key is funded with
its complete observed outgoing amount plus a cushion of one normalised balance
unit. Therefore, both sequential execution and any correct retrying schedule can
commit the full trace. Extra rounds reflect ordering and partitioning effects rather
than arbitrary balance failures. Each regional mempool selects currently ready
sender/nonce entries, commits up to capacity, retains future-nonce transactions,
and retries them after the barrier.
    The complete static sweep uses deterministic seed 0 with k-means n init=10.

The evaluation reports split senders and guarded keys, transactions issued by

token’s ranking and prefix length on the first 70% of the chronological trace,

latency interquartile range. This isolates allocation-induced scan work; it does
not model an index, network delay, or a distributed storage engine.

5     Performance Evaluation
The evaluation examines safety, retry cost, locality, temporal robustness, compo-
nent effects, and comparison with TxAllo. The headline parameters use λg = 0.15
and α = 1.15, selected using D1 as the development trace and then held fixed;
D2, D3, and C1 provide parameter-holdout checks on temporally distinct traces.
Each static allocation is nevertheless fitted and scored on the same trace, so these
are not out-of-sample allocation tests. The static and head-to-head evaluations
cover all six region counts, while the stress, temporal, and mechanism analyses
use n = 6. The operating frontier varies only λg , and each ablation removes one
component at a time. Overall, the static sweep contains 120 trace/region/scheme
measurements, and the balance-stress experiment contains 175 measurements

5.1   Admissibility under Payload Partitioning
TX-KMEANS violates sender locality on every trace and, at larger region counts,
also violates guarded-key locality. Across the 96 sender-local measurements—
ADDR-MOD, both sender k-means variants, and ALPS over four traces and six
region counts—the number of split guarded keys, split senders, and overdrawn
accounts is exactly zero, and every execution commits the complete trace. In
contrast, TX-KMEANS splits 66–1,592 senders and up to 1,334 guarded keys.
Across the four traces and region counts, transactions issued by split senders
account for 10.3–44.4% of the corresponding trace (Fig. ??(a)). Table ?? reports
the detailed results for n = 6.
    The distinction between guarded-key locality and sender locality is clear
at n = 2. Across all four traces, TX-KMEANS splits no guarded keys but
still splits 66–551 senders. With two regions, clustering places complete token
populations on each side, so every (account, token) balance remains region-local
and balance safety is preserved. However, accounts that transact across multiple
tokens can still be divided between regions, creating cross-region dependencies
for the account-wide nonce. This result directly shows the difference between
guarded-key locality and sender locality and is visible because balances are
modelled separately for each token.
    The balance-stress experiment further confirms this behaviour. Across 175
measurements—five traces, seven starting balances, and five schemes—TX-
KMEANS overdraws 6–48 unique accounts in each of its 35 rows, while all
140 sender-local rows record zero overdraws. The lowest-balance tail is non-
monotone because tighter balances can cause regional transfers to fail before
two conflicting debits are both accepted and merged. Therefore, the number of
witnesses is not expected to increase monotonically.

5.6   Comparison with Graph Partitioning and TxAllo

Sender-weighted METIS records zero splits in all 72 runs. Across all 24 trace/region
settings, ALPS lowers its R90 by 18.2–64.4% (mean 39.4%), whereas METIS
achieves a 32.9–91.1% lower interaction cut than ALPS (mean 63.4%) and reaches
a maximum load of 1.058. Thus graph cut optimises interaction locality but does
not optimise semantic retrieval locality. This trade-off arises because METIS
operates on account-interaction topology, whereas R90 depends on token-specific
retrieval concentration.
    The closest algorithmic comparison is the TxAllo allocation objective [?],
which allocates accounts using community detection and jointly optimises cross-
shard traffic and workload balance. This paper reimplements its allocation objec-
tive using Louvain initialisation, candidate-community moves, and coordinate
descent on its throughput score. For shard r, the implementation forms workload
20      Anonymous Author(s)

σr from local transaction count plus (η − 1) times each incident cross-shard
edge weight and greedily moves an account only to a neighbouring community’s
shard when this strictly lowers maxr σr ; ties keep the current shard. It uses
η = 2, at most eight sweeps, and stops when a sweep accepts no move. Pro-
tocol components, including consensus, settlement, brokers, and the A-TxAllo
epoch update, are excluded because they change the execution semantics. The
comparison therefore evaluates allocation objectives under the same execution
model rather than deployed throughput, using η = 2, eight coordinate sweeps,
and three-seed means over eight trace/region settings.

guarded keys in all eight trace/region settings. Thus, this independently designed
workload-aware allocator also satisfies the locality requirement described in
Section ??. The two methods favour different objectives. Relative to ALPS, the
reimplemented objective reduces the active-account cut in all eight trace/region
settings by 13.5–79.2% (mean 50.6%). In contrast, ALPS reduces R90 in all
eight trace/region settings by 40.5–60.8% (mean 47.3%). These results expose
an objective trade-off: the reimplementation assigns greater relative emphasis to
active-account cross-region interaction, whereas ALPS explicitly includes retrieval
locality through R90 . At n = 11, the reimplementation also permits loads of
1.16–1.67, whereas static ALPS maintains its effective load constraint at or below

6    Conclusion and Future Work
For the concurrent same-snapshot additive-merge model, guarded-key locality
is necessary and sufficient for balance non-negativity; the result does not claim
full Ethereum validity or apply to coordinated execution. Sender locality adds
account-nonce order for the evaluated direct calls. The large round-count gap
to transaction clustering measures the cost of sender fragmentation, while the
comparison among admissible allocators exposes the actual ALPS trade-off: better
semantic retrieval locality in exchange for more interaction cut and allocator
time. A query-cost threshold states when that exchange is favourable, and the
in-memory benchmark confirms lower contacted-region and row-scan work.
    Static experiments fit token medians on the complete allocation trace. The
chronological replay instead fits them on the earliest 50% prefix and freezes
them for all later transactions, removing suffix leakage; its declared horizon still
trades interaction cut against realised load and provides no lifetime cap. The
evidence remains limited to short submitted-call workloads from two EVM chains.
Direct calls exclude routers and other nonce-consuming transactions, receipts
and historical balances are not reconstructed, and the query benchmark does not
emulate distributed storage. TxAllo covers its allocation objective, not its protocol,
while barrier rounds measure scheduling efficiency rather than production TPS.
Future work will add receipt-aware archive replay, diverse read/write sets, indexed
distributed retrieval, migration, and protocol integration.
