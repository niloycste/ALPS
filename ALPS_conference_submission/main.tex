\begin{abstract}
In independent-barrier execution, regions validate against one starting state
and merge accepted changes additively. Payload clustering can then split debits
of one balance across regions. We prove that guarded-key locality is necessary
and sufficient for balance non-negativity in this model. ALPS enforces the
stronger sender locality required by account-wide Ethereum nonces and optimises
semantic locality, active-account interaction cut, and historical load.

Across four direct-call traces from Ethereum and BNB Smart Chain (145,372
submitted calls), ALPS improves retrieval locality and interaction cut over
address hashing in all 24 trace/region settings. Compared with balanced sender
$k$-means, it lowers the interaction cut in all 24 settings by
$12.8$--$82.5\%$; $R_{90}$ improves in 19 settings, ties in two, and is worse in
three. In a prospective 70/30 holdout, ALPS scans 4,404 rows at 90.78\% suffix
recall, compared with 8,312 rows at 88.46\% for METIS$+$C, but has a higher
interaction cut (.592 versus .097) and load (1.534 versus 1.191). Interpolation
of frozen plans at 90\% recall estimates 4,419 rows for ALPS and
7,139--9,020 for the comparators. These scan-work results support ALPS for
retrieval-oriented workloads whose savings justify its interaction, load, and
allocation costs; they do not establish end-to-end latency gains.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

State-based blockchains often process transactions in sequence because validity
depends on shared state~\cite{wood2014}. Region-based parallel mining~\cite{chishti2021} divides pending transactions among independent regions.
Each region processes its assigned transactions, and the regional blocks are
joined at a barrier. This approach can increase parallel work, but it also
creates a placement problem. During each round, regions validate transactions
against the same committed state. Two regions may therefore accept debits that
become unsafe when their changes are merged.

Semantic sharding improves retrieval~\cite{zhen2024semantichain}, while
workload-aware placement reduces cross-shard traffic
~\cite{txallo2023,wang2024orbit,zhang2025mosaic} and conflict-oriented systems
change ordering or scheduling~\cite{hong2023prophet,liu2026concordia}. These
lines do not characterise admissible placement for independent barriers.

Grouping calls by payload can keep related token operations close and reduce
the number of regions scanned by an asset query. However, payload describes the
operation, not the balance that it uses. Two regions may validate debits of the
same account-token balance against the same starting state. The merged balance
can then become negative. We call the required same-region rule
\emph{guarded-key locality}. Ethereum's transaction nonce is account-wide, not
token-specific. Keeping each guarded key local therefore does not always keep
the sender's nonce order local. ALPS uses the stronger rule of assigning all
calls from one sender to one region.

The main contributions of this paper are:

\begin{itemize}
\item We characterise balance-safe partitions for concurrent, same-snapshot
regional execution with additive merging and no cross-region serialization:
all debits of each guarded key must be region-local.

\item We present ALPS, a sender-indivisible allocator that combines weighted
semantic prototypes, an interaction-graph cut, and load control under a hard
static historical-load constraint. Exact whole-sender moves give monotone local
refinement; a chronological extension pins new senders.

\item We evaluate safety, scheduling, retrieval, interaction, load, and
allocation cost on four direct-call traces (145,372 calls) and a separate
17,445-event log trace, including prospective replay, ablations, METIS, and a
reimplementation of the TxAllo allocation objective.
\end{itemize}

The novelty lies in deriving the admissible design space, separating guarded-key
safety from account-nonce locality, and making that space a hard constraint on
joint semantic, interaction, and load optimisation. Sender grouping, clustering,
graph partitioning, and chronological pinning are established techniques and
are not individual novelty claims. The theorem is confined to uncoordinated
same-snapshot additive merging; coordinated execution can admit other
partitions.

\section{Related Work}

The independent regional model~\cite{chishti2021} differs from conflict
re-execution~\cite{dickerson2017,gelashvili2023}, static parallelism
analysis~\cite{bartoletti2021theory}, dependency schedules
~\cite{amiri2019parblockchain,chen2021peep}, speculation~\cite{xu2025vegeta},
object-level independence~\cite{blackshear2024sui}, coordinated
ordering~\cite{hong2023prophet,liu2026concordia}, and cross-shard coordination
~\cite{amiri2021sharper,monoxide2019}. Our admissibility result concerns only
the uncoordinated barrier model.

SemantiChain couples semantic placement with retrieval~\cite{zhen2024semantichain};
ALPS adds a model-derived admissibility constraint and explicit interaction and
load terms.

Workload-aware placement is the closest algorithmic line. OptChain uses an
online graph~\cite{optchain2019}; Shard Scheduler migrates objects
~\cite{shardscheduler2021}; BrokerChain uses broker accounts
~\cite{brokerchain2022}; TxAllo optimises traffic and balance
~\cite{txallo2023}; and Orbit and Mosaic adapt account placement
~\cite{wang2024orbit,zhang2025mosaic}. Section~\ref{sec:txallo} compares ALPS
with the TxAllo allocation objective. Because A-TxAllo already places new nodes
over time, our chronological experiment tests robustness rather than precedence.

Database partitioning provides another useful comparison. Schism balances a
co-access graph to reduce distributed transactions~\cite{curino2010}. ALPS
follows a similar idea, but it moves only whole senders so that the resulting
allocation remains admissible without abort or retry coordination. We also
apply weighted multilevel METIS~\cite{karypis1998metis} to the active-sender
graph. This comparison separates a general graph-cut objective from ALPS's
semantic term and effective load constraint.

\section{Proposed Work}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{System Model and Admissibility}
\label{sec:model}

The model has two separate locality requirements. Guarded-key locality
protects balances, while sender locality keeps account-nonce dependencies within
one region.

\subsubsection{Independent-barrier Execution}

Let $T$ be a transaction set and
$P:T\rightarrow\{1,\ldots,n\}$ its static region assignment. At the start of
round $k$, every region reads the same committed state $\sigma_k$. Each region
executes concurrently with the others, processes its transactions sequentially
on a private copy, and accepts a debit only if its local balance is sufficient;
an insufficient-balance debit is rejected in that execution. Regions neither
observe nor serialize with one another during the round. At the barrier, all
accepted debit and credit deltas are merged additively to form $\sigma_{k+1}$.
This follows the execution structure of the base
parallel-mining design~\cite{chishti2021}; proof-of-work is independent of the
state argument considered here.

An ERC-20 balance is associated with both an account and a token contract. Let
$b=(a,c)$ denote the balance of account $a$ in token contract $c$. A transfer
$t=(a,d,c,x,q)$ conditionally consumes $x$ from debit key
$g(t)=(a,c)$, credits $(d,c)$, and carries account-wide nonce $q$. Credits are
unconditional additions, whereas debits are guarded non-commutative
operations.

For balance safety, all transactions that debit the same guarded key must stay
in the same region. This requirement, called \emph{guarded-key locality}, can
be written as
\[
g(t_i)=g(t_j)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad P(t_i)=P(t_j).
\]
A partition is \emph{balance-safe barrier-admissible} if, for every non-negative
starting balance vector and every same-round transfer workload, independently
validated deltas cannot merge into a negative balance. This property concerns
balance non-negativity only, not nonce validity, completeness, or liveness.

\begin{theorem}[Balance-Safety Admissibility]
\label{thm:barrier_admissibility}
Under the concurrent same-snapshot, additive-merge execution just defined,
without cross-region serialization or revalidation, a static transfer
partition is balance-safe barrier-admissible for every non-negative starting
balance vector and same-round workload if and only if it satisfies guarded-key
locality.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For sufficiency, consider a guarded key $b$ in round $k$. Guarded-key locality
implies that only one region $r$ can debit $b$. Sequential local validation in
that region gives
\[
D_r(b)\leq \sigma_k(b)+C_r(b),
\]
where $D_r(b)$ and $C_r(b)$ are the debit and credit totals of region $r$.
After the regional changes are merged,
\[
\sigma_{k+1}(b)
=\sigma_k(b)+\sum_j C_j(b)-D_r(b)
\geq \sum_{j\neq r}C_j(b)\geq0.
\]
Thus, the merged balance cannot become negative, and the same argument applies
inductively over subsequent rounds.

For necessity, suppose the static assignment places two debits of the same
guarded key in different regions in the same round, with
positive debit totals $d_1$ and $d_2$, and no credits are made to that key. If
the starting balance is $\max(d_1,d_2)$, both regions can accept their debits
because they validate against the same starting state. After the regional
changes are merged, the balance becomes
\[
\max(d_1,d_2)-d_1-d_2<0.
\]
Hence any such partition fails to be balance-safe barrier-admissible for some
starting balance and same-round workload.
\end{proof}

The necessity direction is specific to uncoordinated independent barriers.
Coordination, revalidation, or re-execution can prevent the simultaneous
acceptance used by the counterexample; guarded-key locality remains sufficient
in those systems, but need not be necessary.

\subsubsection{Sender Locality for Global Nonces}

Ethereum assigns a transaction nonce to the sending account, not to a token.
If one sender is split across regions, a transaction with a later nonce may
have to wait for an earlier transaction to commit in another region. ALPS
therefore assigns all transactions from sender $a$, across all tokens, to one
region. For the direct calls in our evaluation, the outer signer is also the
token debitor. Sender locality is therefore stronger than guarded-key locality
for these calls and keeps both token-balance updates and account-nonce order in
one region. Calls from one signer that debit different tokens illustrate the
distinction: their guarded keys may be separated safely by
Theorem~\ref{thm:barrier_admissibility},
while consecutive account nonces still require ordered progress. Router-mediated
calls and arbitrary contract read/write sets fall outside this implication.

\begin{corollary}[Direct-Call Locality]
\label{cor:sender_locality}
For the evaluated direct ERC-20 calls, where the outer signer is the debited
token holder, sender locality implies guarded-key locality and makes every
submitted account-nonce dependency region-local.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
All guarded keys $(a,c)$ belonging to sender $a$ map to the sender's single
region, and every submitted transaction carrying $a$'s account nonce maps to
that same region.
\end{proof}

A nonce-aware mempool can defer a future-nonce transaction and retry it after
lower nonces commit, restoring eventual progress at the cost of extra barriers.
Section~\ref{sec:results} measures this cost and separately stress-tests
guarded-key balance safety.

\subsection{Proposed Method: ALPS}
\label{sec:alps}

ALPS uses the sender as its allocation unit. It combines semantic locality,
interaction locality, and historical-load control. We first describe its
static allocation method and then its fixed-horizon chronological extension.

\subsubsection{Allocation Unit and Objective}

ALPS treats each active sender $a$ and its complete transaction sequence as one
indivisible unit. Let $w_a$ denote its historical transaction count, $x_a$ its
mean payload feature vector, and $e_{ab}$ the symmetrized number of observed
submitted calls in either direction between active senders $a$ and $b$. Define $W=\sum_a w_a$,
$M=\sum_{\{a,b\}}e_{ab}$, the mean regional load $\bar L=W/n$, and
$L_r=\sum_{a:z_a=r}w_a$. The exact dimensionless objective is

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:objective}
\begin{aligned}
J={}&\frac{1}{W}\sum_a w_a
 \frac{\lVert x_a-\mu_{z_a}\rVert_2^2}{s}\\
 &+\frac{\lambda_g}{M}\sum_{\{a,b\}}e_{ab}\mathbf{1}[z_a\ne z_b]\\
 &+\frac{\lambda_l}{n}\sum_{r=1}^{n}\left(\frac{L_r}{\bar L}\right)^2
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The heuristic reduces this objective while keeping each sender in one region
and enforcing $L_r\leq C_{\mathrm{eff}}$. A feasible cap cannot be smaller than
the largest sender, so the target is
$C_0=\max(\alpha\bar L,\max_a w_a)$. If a whole-sender packing satisfies
$C_0$, ALPS uses $C_{\mathrm{eff}}=C_0$. Otherwise, the LPT fallback provides
a feasible packing, and the effective cap is raised only to the maximum load
of that packing. In every reported run, $\max_a w_a\leq\alpha\bar L$ and no
relaxation was needed. Thus, $C_0=C_{\mathrm{eff}}=\alpha\bar L$ in these runs.

The scale $s$ is computed once, after the initial weighted $k$-means fit. It is
the median nonzero squared distance from each active-sender profile to each
initial prototype. The same value is used during placement, prototype updates,
and refinement. The experiments use $\alpha=1.15$, $\lambda_g=0.15$, and
$\lambda_l=0.20$. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the graph-weight frontier;
therefore, $\lambda_g=0.15$ is not presented as a universal optimum. The
implementation requires $W>0$ and at least $n$ active senders. It replaces
zero values of $M$ or $s$ with one, keeps the previous prototype for an empty
region, and resolves equal-cost choices by selecting the lowest region index.

The best operating point depends on the deployment. Let $X$ be the
active-account interaction cut defined in Section~\ref{sec:method}. A
deployment may combine retrieval cost and interaction cost in the score
$D_\kappa=R_{90}+\kappa X$. Here, $\kappa$ expresses the interaction-cut
penalty in $R_{90}$-equivalent units. Under this score, ALPS is preferable to a
baseline $B$ when

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:deployment}
\kappa > \kappa^\star=
\frac{R_{90}^{\textsc{alps}}-R_{90}^{B}}{X_B-X_{\textsc{alps}}}
\end{equation}

provided $X_{\textsc{alps}}<X_B$. A non-positive threshold means that ALPS
weakly dominates. This formulation makes the retrieval--interaction trade-off
explicit instead of hiding it inside a single composite score.

The objective has three parts. The payload-distance term supports
asset-query locality. The graph term penalises interactions between active
accounts in different regions. The load term penalises uneven historical load.
A separate hard constraint limits the maximum historical load and is distinct
from the per-round block capacity $B$. Sender indivisibility is a hard rule
outside the objective, so the optimiser cannot split a sender to reduce the
objective.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:alps} gives the static heuristic. If greedy seeding exceeds
$C_0$, largest-processing-time-first (LPT) packing provides a feasible cap for
the refinement stage. No reported run required this relaxation. Proposition~1
shows that refinement preserves feasibility and reduces the objective; it
does not show that ALPS finds a global optimum.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Static ALPS: all assignments and moves keep senders whole.}
\label{alg:alps}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE $T_h,n,\alpha,\lambda_g,\lambda_l$; objective $J$ in~\eqref{eq:objective}
\STATE Compute sender profiles $(w_a,x_a)$ and interaction weights $e_{ab}$
\STATE Initialise prototypes by transaction-weighted $k$-means;
 $L\leftarrow0$, $z\leftarrow\emptyset$
\STATE Set $s$ from the initial prototypes and hold it fixed
\STATE $C_0\leftarrow\max(\alpha W/n,\max_a w_a)$
\FOR{senders $a$ ordered by decreasing $w_a$, then account ID}
\STATE $F\leftarrow\{r:L_r+w_a\leq C_0\}$; if empty,
 $F\leftarrow\{\arg\min_r L_r\}$
\STATE Assign $a$ to the region in $F$ with least incremental $J$
 against placed senders; update $L$
\ENDFOR
\IF{$\max_r L_r>C_0$}
\STATE Restart packing in the same sender order, assigning each sender to
 the least-loaded region
\ENDIF
\STATE $C_{\rm eff}\leftarrow\max(C_0,\max_r L_r)$
\FOR{at most six sweeps}
\STATE Recompute transaction-weighted prototypes; retain empty ones
\FOR{senders $a$ in the same order}
\STATE Find the feasible destination minimising exact $\Delta J$;
 move $a$ and update $L$ only if $\Delta J<-10^{-12}$
\ENDFOR
\STATE Stop if no sender moved
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $z$ (ties retain the current region, otherwise lowest region ID)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Static Time Complexity}
Let $N=|T_h|$, $A$ be the active senders, $E$ their interaction edges, $I$ the
weighted $k$-means iterations, and $S\leq6$ the refinement sweeps. Profiling
costs $O(Nd)$, weighted $k$-means $O(InAd)$, sender sorting $O(A\log A)$, and
the seed or one sweep $O(n(Ad+E))$. Thus worst-case static time is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:static_complexity}
O\!\left(Nd + InAd + A\log A + (S+1)n(Ad+E)\right).
\end{equation}
LPT adds $O(A\log A+A\log n)$ if invoked; no reported run required it.
Allocator storage excluding the input is $O(Ad+E+n)$.

\begin{proposition}[Monotone Constrained Refinement]
With fixed prototypes, every accepted ALPS move strictly decreases
\eqref{eq:objective} while preserving sender locality and the effective
capacity constraint. Replacing each prototype by its transaction-weighted mean
cannot increase the semantic term.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Each accepted whole-sender move has negative exact $\Delta J$ and a feasible
destination, preserving locality and capacity. The positive normalisation $s$
is fixed throughout refinement. For fixed assignments, weighted means minimise
the semantic numerator; division by the same $s$ therefore preserves the
non-increase, so prototype updates cannot increase $J$.
The six-sweep limit may stop refinement before a one-move local optimum.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection{Fixed-Horizon Chronological Placement}

Senders seen in the history keep their assigned regions. When an unseen
sender $a$ first appears in the future stream, ALPS selects a region and pins
that sender there for the rest of the replay. The selected region is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:chronological_score}
\begin{aligned}
z_a&=\arg\min_{r\in F_t}\{\|x_t-\mu_r\|_2^2/s_t+\lambda_g e_{tr}
 +\lambda_l\ell_r^2\},\\
e_{tr}&=\mathbf{1}[\rho_t\neq\bot\ \land\ r\neq\rho_t],\qquad
\ell_r=L_r/\max(C_f,1).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $x_t$ uses frozen prefix scaling, $\mu_r$ is the frozen
transaction-weighted history prototype, and $\rho_t$ is the receiver's region
if already assigned, otherwise $\bot$. Both numerator and scale use squared
Euclidean distance: $s_t=\operatorname{median}\{\|x_t-\mu_j\|_2^2:
\|x_t-\mu_j\|_2^2>10^{-12}\}$, or one if the set is empty. The semantic
ratio is therefore dimensionless, matching the implementation.
With $C_f=\alpha H_f/n$, $F_t=\{r:L_r+1\leq C_f\}$, falling back to all
regions if empty. Loads start at zero for the suffix, increase after every
routed transaction, and ties choose the lowest region ID. The semantic weight
is one in the reported runs.

This experiment uses a fixed horizon, with $H_f=|T_f|$ predefined for the
replay. Given this horizon, each placement decision uses only the payload and
placements observed so far. The capacity check is made only when a sender is
first placed. Later activity from that sender can raise the regional load above
$C_f$, so this rule does not provide a lifetime hard-cap guarantee.

\paragraph{Chronological Time Complexity}
Hash-map routing takes $O(1)$ expected time for a pinned sender and $O(nd)$
for each first placement. For $F$ future transactions and $U$ unseen senders,
total time is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:online_complexity}
O(F+Und),
\end{equation}
and the assignment map grows by $O(U)$ entries.

A-TxAllo already carries allocations forward and places new nodes in a 9:1
chronological split~\cite{txallo2023}; our four-horizon replay is a robustness
check. All replay baselines preserve history assignments and pin unseen senders
using their own rules. ALPS's replay replaces the static graph term with the
currently placed receiver indicator in~\eqref{eq:chronological_score}.

\subsection{Traces and Feature Construction}

Table~\ref{tab:data} lists three Ethereum mainnet traces covering eight
ERC-20 contracts and one held-out BNB Smart Chain (BSC) trace covering a
separate set of eight tokens. D1--D3 contain only direct
\texttt{transfer(address,uint256)} calls. For these calls, the outer signer is
both the debited token owner and the account-nonce owner. We convert observed
signer nonces to ranks within each sender; this keeps their order unchanged.
Because other account transactions are not included, these traces represent
the collected direct-transfer stream, not the full Ethereum mempool. The traces
cover non-overlapping periods on 14, 13, and 7 August 2026. D3 is approximately
one week before the other two Ethereum traces. The D2, D3, and C1 manifests
record complete scans of their requested inclusive spans. The original D1
acquisition did not retain a complete scanned-block ledger, so its scan
coverage cannot be established from the artifact.

The selector, calldata, and nonce fields have the same meaning on both
chains. C1 is a difficult case for semantic placement: one token accounts for

separate \texttt{Transfer}-log trace adds more balance events, but it does not
identify the outer signer for router-mediated calls. We therefore do not use
this trace for nonce-related claims. The artifact manifests list the block
ranges, chain IDs, contract addresses, filters, hashes, RPC endpoints, and
data provenance.

We collect the traces through public JSON-RPC endpoints. For D2, D3, and C1,
the complete-scan collectors request every height in the stated span, store
transaction hashes, and abort if any block response is missing. Their manifests
separately record the number scanned. The Blocks/span numerator in
Table~\ref{tab:data} counts only heights containing at least one retained row;
therefore 713/714, 499/500, and 2489/2500 do not indicate missing block
responses. D1 lacks that complete ledger and is reported only by its 513
represented heights. We exclude eleven self-transfers from the log trace,
leaving 17,445 events (Table~\ref{tab:data}). For the direct-call traces, we
keep calls to the listed token contracts that use the standard selector, have
nonzero decodable calldata, and name a recipient other than the sender. We
exclude router and proxy calls, and remove duplicate sender nonces during
loading.

The main experiments do not filter calls by receipt status. They therefore
include submitted calls that may later have reverted. In this paper, a
``call'' does not mean that a token state change succeeded. A reverted
submission may still use a nonce and affect the locality metrics.

Raw ERC-20 values cannot be compared directly across tokens because token
decimals and price scales differ. For transaction $t$ with raw value $v_t$ and
token $c$, let
$\rho_t=\operatorname{clip}(v_t/\operatorname{median}_{j:c_j=c}v_j,0.1,20)$.
The exact clustering feature is
$x_t=[4e_c;\log(1+\rho_t)]$, where $e_c$ is the token one-hot vector; a sender
profile is the arithmetic mean of its transactions' $x_t$ vectors. The balance
stress model uses transfer amount $8\rho_t$. We do not add Gaussian noise or a
synthetic clustering coordinate. Thus, when transaction $k$-means splits a
dominant token, the split is based on an observed difference in transaction
amounts.

For static allocation, we calculate token medians from the full allocation
trace. For each fixed-horizon temporal split, we calculate the medians from
the first 50\% of the trace and keep them fixed for both history and suffix
transactions. No suffix amount is used to scale temporal features. This
common prefix scale keeps feature dimensions comparable across the reported
50--80\% history fractions. The temporal horizon is a planning assumption and
is varied around the observed suffix length in a separate sensitivity test.
A production streaming allocator would need to update these statistics
online and choose its planning horizon independently.

\subsection{Schemes and Execution}

The main static evaluation compares ADDR-MOD, TX-KMEANS, the sender-based
$k$-means variants, and ALPS. It does not include METIS or TxAllo; these methods
are compared separately in Section~\ref{sec:txallo}. ADDR-MOD assigns a sender
using $a\bmod n$. TX-KMEANS clusters individual transactions, while the
sender-based methods cluster the mean features of each active sender.
S-KM-BAL also applies the $1.15\times$ historical-load target. ALPS uses the
joint objective described in Section~\ref{sec:alps}.

We test $n\in\{2,3,4,6,8,11\}$ regions and set the capacity of each region
to 250 transactions. In the global-nonce experiment, each
(account, token) debit key starts with its total observed outgoing amount plus
a cushion of one normalised balance unit. This allows sequential execution and
any correct retry schedule to commit the full trace. The extra rounds therefore
measure ordering and partitioning effects, not failures caused by insufficient
starting balances. Each regional mempool selects ready sender/nonce entries,
commits up to its capacity, keeps future-nonce transactions, and retries them
after the barrier.

The full static sweep uses deterministic seed 0 and $k$-means
\texttt{n\_init=10}. The temporal, frontier, ablation, and TxAllo comparisons
use three initialisations. We base allocation-sensitive conclusions on these
sensitivity runs as well as on the exact static audit. The capacity and
allocator-runtime audits use five fresh initialisations.

For the balance-safety experiment, we give each observed (account, token)
key the same starting balance. We test seven values:
$\{1000,600,400,250,150,100,60\}$. We ignore account nonces only in this
experiment so that we can test simultaneous debits of one guarded key. A
negative balance after the synthetic merge is an exact witness of a balance
violation in that run. It is not a reconstruction of historical execution.
Balances from different token contracts are never combined.

The state and scheduling experiments set proof-of-work difficulty to zero.
We report barrier rounds and the schedule-level commit rate
$Q_s=N_c/R$, where $N_c$ is the number of committed transactions and $R$ is
the number of rounds. This measure is not wall-clock transactions per second
(TPS). Given regional capacity $B$, the ratio $Q_s/(nB)$ measures schedule
utilisation. It helps isolate dependency stalls from hashing, storage,
networking, and consensus. The simulator also has a SHA-256 path for separate
benchmarking, but we make no wall-clock claim based on it.

\subsection{Metrics and Temporal Protocol}

We report split senders and guarded keys, the share of transactions issued
by split senders, barrier rounds, deferred nonce checks, maximum-to-mean
regional load, and the active-account interaction cut. For an allocation, let
$T_{\mathrm{act}}$ contain the submitted calls whose receiver also appears as
an active sender. The interaction cut is
\[
X=\frac{|\{t\in T_{\mathrm{act}}:z_{\mathrm{sender}(t)}\neq
z_{\mathrm{receiver}(t)}\}|}{|T_{\mathrm{act}}|},
\]
Interaction coverage is $|T_{\mathrm{act}}|/|T|$. Receiver-only addresses
are not included in the cut and are not automatically counted as
cross-region. We report regional load as $\max_r L_r/\bar L$. To measure
retrieval locality, we rank regions for each token by the number of calls or
events for that token. $R_{90}$ is the number of top-ranked regions needed to
cover at least 90\% of the token's calls or events, averaged across the
evaluated tokens. A lower $R_{90}$ means that fewer regions need to be scanned;
it does not mean that measured query latency is lower. We also run an
in-memory benchmark. It stores each region as a category array and tests
three types of query. \emph{Oracle-90}
ranks regions using the evaluated records and scans the minimum prefix covering
at least 90\%; it is an approximate, in-sample diagnostic and intentionally
omits the remaining records. \emph{History-90} learns each token's ranking and
prefix length on the first 70\% of the chronological trace, freezes that plan,
and reports achieved recall on the final 30\%. \emph{Complete} scans all regions
and returns every match. Allocations remain the static full-trace allocations
used in the head-to-head comparison, so History-90 tests the query planner, not
out-of-sample allocator fitting. After warm-up, 250 repetitions per token record
contacted regions, examined rows, result recall, median latency, and latency
interquartile range. This benchmark measures allocation-related scan work. It does not model an
index, network delay, or a distributed storage engine. History-90 excludes
tokens that do not appear in the history: there are none on D1--D3, and C1's
BUSD token has one suffix call. Each method and seed therefore covers
eight/eight/eight/seven token categories. There are no categories that appear
only in the history.

\paragraph{Prospective Allocation and Queries}
\label{sec:prospective_protocol}
We also run a separate 70/30 chronological holdout. Token medians, vocabulary,
sender profiles, the interaction graph, allocation, prototypes, and query plans
are fitted using the history only. Address IDs are assigned when they first
appear. We set the future horizon to $H_f=\lceil0.5|T_h|\rceil$ before
evaluation; it is not chosen from the suffix size. ALPS assigns new senders
using~\eqref{eq:chronological_score}, while S-KM-BAL does not use the receiver
term. Our causal versions of METIS and TxAllo use
$0.15e_{tr}+0.20\ell_r^2$, with the same candidate set and sender-pinning
rule. These are our adaptations, not the original streaming protocols.

Unseen tokens use a fixed out-of-vocabulary feature coordinate and the frozen
history-wide median raw amount as a fallback scale. This is a heuristic; the
fallback does not give amounts from different tokens a common economic
meaning. Queries for unseen tokens scan all regions, so all suffix tokens are
included. Six regions, three seeds, and seven preset coverage targets produce
48 allocations and 2,688 token-level query measurements. For the matched-recall
comparison, we first average tokens within each trace, method, and seed. We
then linearly interpolate rows scanned and regions contacted at suffix recalls
of $\{.80,.85,.90,.95\}$ between adjacent preset plans, and average the 12
configurations. This interpolation is descriptive: suffix results do not
select a coverage target or a deployed plan. Per-trace seed results are
provided in the artifact.

Static interaction coverage depends only on the trace and is therefore
strategy-invariant: $53.8\%$ for D1, $56.6\%$ for D2, $60.6\%$ for D3, and
$45.7\%$ for C1. This conditional metric matches ALPS's active-sender graph
while keeping the denominator explicit.

The fixed-horizon replay uses $n=6$, history fractions
$\{0.5,0.6,0.7,0.8\}$, and three $k$-means initialisations. This gives 12
history/future configurations for each method. The figures show the mean and
standard deviation across these configurations. They are sensitivity summaries,
not independent samples from the chains. Across the four traces, 60.0\% of
future transactions come from senders that are not in the training prefix.
This makes the treatment of new senders important. At a history fraction of
$0.7$, we also test four ALPS horizons:
$H_f/|T_f|\in\{0.5,1,1.5,2\}$, on every direct-call trace and seed.

\section{Performance Evaluation}
\label{sec:results}

We evaluate balance safety, retry cost, locality, temporal robustness,
component effects, and the comparisons with graph partitioning and TxAllo.
The main parameters are $\lambda_g=0.15$ and $\alpha=1.15$. We select them
using D1 as the development trace and then keep them fixed. D2, D3, and C1
provide checks on temporally separate traces. However, each static allocation
is fitted and evaluated on the same trace, so these tests are not out-of-sample
allocation tests. The static and head-to-head evaluations use all six region
counts. The stress, temporal, and mechanism analyses use $n=6$. The operating
frontier varies only $\lambda_g$, and each ablation removes one component.
The static sweep contains 120 trace/region/scheme measurements. The
balance-stress experiment contains 175 measurements: 140 sender-local and
35 TX-KMEANS.

\subsection{Admissibility under Payload Partitioning}

TX-KMEANS splits senders on every trace and, when the number of regions is
larger, it also splits guarded keys. Across the 96 sender-local measurements
(ADDR-MOD, both sender $k$-means methods, and ALPS over four traces and six
region counts), there are no split senders, no split guarded keys, and no
overdrawn accounts. Every method commits the full trace. By contrast,
TX-KMEANS splits 66--1{,}592 senders and up to 1{,}334 guarded keys. Across the
four traces and region counts, transactions from split senders make up
$10.3$--$44.4\%$ of each trace (Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep}(a)). Table~\ref{tab:headline}
shows the results for $n=6$.

The difference between guarded-key locality and sender locality is clear
when $n=2$. Across all four traces, TX-KMEANS does not split guarded keys, but
it still splits 66--551 senders. With two regions, clustering places each
token's transactions on one side. As a result, every
$(\text{account},\text{token})$ balance stays in one region, so balance safety
is preserved. However, an account that uses several tokens can still be split
between regions. This creates cross-region dependencies for its account-wide
nonce. The result shows why guarded-key locality alone does not ensure sender
locality.

The balance-stress experiment gives further evidence. It contains 175
measurements: five traces, seven starting balances, and five methods. In each
of the 35 TX-KMEANS cases, 6--48 unique accounts are overdrawn. All 140
sender-local cases have zero overdrawn accounts. The results at the lowest
starting balances are not monotone. With a smaller balance, a regional
transfer may fail before two conflicting debits are both accepted and merged.
The number of balance-safety witnesses therefore does not have to increase as
the starting balance decreases.

\subsection{Retry Cost of Sender Fragmentation}

Deferring and retrying future-nonce transactions allows the full trace to
commit, but it does not remove the scheduling cost. After retries, every method
commits $100\%$ of each trace with no pending transactions. However, TX-KMEANS
needs 156--1{,}760 rounds, compared with 11--106 rounds for ALPS. It also
performs up to $3.13$ million future-nonce checks. Its schedule-level commit
rate, $Q_s$, falls to 22--253 transactions per round, compared with
432--2{,}338 for ALPS.

At $n=6$, TX-KMEANS needs about $40\times$, $45\times$, $24\times$, and
$24\times$ as many barrier rounds as ALPS on D1, D2, D3, and C1, respectively
(Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep}(b), Table~\ref{tab:headline}). This comparison measures
the scheduling cost of splitting senders whose transactions have an account-
nonce order. It is not a speedup over the strongest admissible method: ALPS and
balanced sender $k$-means both need 27 rounds on D1. The claim for ALPS is
instead that, among sender-local methods, it offers a different balance
between retrieval locality and interaction locality under the same load
constraint.

\subsection{Locality and Load Trade-offs}

Compared with ADDR-MOD, ALPS improves both locality measures in all 24
trace/region settings, while keeping each sender in one region. The additional
cost is allocator computation (Section~\ref{sec:components}). $R_{90}$
decreases by $18.8$--$68.5\%$ (mean $45.9\%$), and the interaction cut
decreases by $35.2$--$75.9\%$ (mean $57.7\%$). The static load does not exceed
the $1.15$ target in any reported run.

A more useful comparison is with balanced sender $k$-means, which also
satisfies the locality requirements. ALPS reduces the interaction cut in all
24 settings by $12.8$--$82.5\%$ (mean $50.9\%$). Its retrieval measure,
$R_{90}$, improves in 19 settings, is unchanged in two, and is worse in three.
The three cases occur at $n=6$ on D1 and D2 and at $n=4$ on D3. Only the D1
case uses the development trace for parameter selection. We report these
cases to show that ALPS does not improve every measure in every setting.

In most settings, ALPS improves both measures. In the other settings, the
break-even ratio $\kappa^\star$ shows when the simpler method may be a better
choice under the score in~\eqref{eq:deployment}. For D1 with $n=6$,
$\kappa^\star=1.94$. A value above this threshold gives more weight to
interaction cut and favours ALPS; a lower value favours SENDER-KMEANS-BAL.
At larger region counts, ALPS often weakly dominates, so the threshold is
non-positive.

Figure~\ref{fig:locality} helps explain why the three retrieval exceptions
occur at smaller values of $n$. As the number of regions increases, the
retrieval cost of ADDR-MOD rises. This gives semantic partitioning more room
to improve $R_{90}$. At $n=11$, ALPS lowers $R_{90}$ by $10.0$--$37.8\%$
compared with balanced sender $k$-means across all four traces. Unconstrained
SENDER-KMEANS also gives strong semantic locality, but its load reaches $4.45$
at $n=11$. The capacity-aware methods remain at or below $1.15$.

\subsection{Component Analysis and Allocator Overhead}
\label{sec:components}

Removing the semantic term raises $R_{90}$ to
$5.00/5.25/4.88/4.12$ on D1/D2/D3/C1, while it lowers the interaction cut.
Removing the capacity constraint raises load to $1.28$--$1.52$, above the
measured $1.15$ maximum-imbalance target. Setting $\lambda_g=0$ removes the
interaction term. This variant is not identical to balanced sender $k$-means:
it uses transaction-weighted prototypes and exact objective refinement.

The static graph-weight curves may flatten or reverse above $0.35$. This is
consistent with the non-convex search. We do not claim one universal best
weight.

A five-seed audit tests
$\alpha\in\{1.05,1.10,1.15,1.25,1.50\}$ with six regions on all four traces.
No run raises its requested cap. Increasing $\alpha$ from 1.05 to 1.15 lowers
the cut on every trace. Larger values produce different, non-monotone changes
across traces. The error intervals in the artifact measure variation across
initialisations, not variation across workloads.

Allocator computation is noticeable at this scale. For D1 prefixes of
5,000, 10,000, 20,000, and 34,326 calls, fresh single-worker runs over three
seeds take median profile-and-allocation times of 2.50, 5.32, 8.08, and
12.60~s on an Intel i9-14900. On the full trace, which has 18,747 active
senders and 16,120 interaction edges, the peak process working set is
248.4~MiB. No run raises the capacity bound. These measurements describe
allocator overhead; they are not production TPS measurements.

A separate comparison uses five seeds and fresh processes with six regions.
The trace-wise median times are $<0.01$~s for ADDR-MOD,
$0.16$--$0.26$~s for TX-KMEANS, $0.29$--$0.68$~s for S-KM-BAL,
$0.13$--$0.28$~s for METIS, $4.17$--$6.88$~s for TxAllo, and
$10.99$--$15.74$~s for ALPS. CSV loading is excluded. The times include the
profile or graph construction required by each method.

The TxAllo comparator~\cite{txallo2023} is a reimplementation of its
allocation objective. It uses Louvain initialisation, candidate-community moves,
and coordinate descent on the throughput score. For each shard $r$, workload
$\sigma_r$ is the local transaction count plus $(\eta-1)$ times the weight of
incident cross-shard edges. An account moves only to a neighbouring
community's shard when the move strictly lowers $\max_r\sigma_r$. Ties keep
the current assignment. We set $\eta=2$ and stop after eight sweeps or after
a sweep with no accepted move. The results are means over three seeds and
eight trace/region settings. We exclude consensus, settlement, brokers, and
A-TxAllo epoch updates. This is a comparison of allocation objectives under
one execution model, not a comparison of deployed throughput.

The reimplementation assigns each account to one shard and records no split
senders or guarded keys in the eight trace/region settings. It therefore also
meets the locality condition in Section~\ref{sec:model}. The two methods,
however, favour different objectives. Compared with ALPS, the reimplemented
objective lowers the active-account cut in all eight settings by
$16.7$--$70.2\%$ (mean $45.0\%$). ALPS lowers $R_{90}$ in all eight settings
by $38.1$--$60.8\%$ (mean $46.0\%$). These percentages are relative changes
computed from the unrounded three-seed mean of each metric in each trace/region
setting; they are not averages of seed-wise percentage changes. The
reimplementation gives more weight
to cross-region interactions. ALPS also includes a semantic-distance term to
improve retrieval locality, which we measure using $R_{90}$. At $n=11$, the
reimplementation allows loads of $1.16$--$1.67$. Static ALPS keeps its
effective load at or below $1.15$.

We use a simple cost model to describe when the retrieval benefit may
justify the higher interaction cut. For one submitted transaction, let $q$ be
the number of token queries drawn uniformly across the evaluated categories,
as in $R_{90}$. Let $c_q$ be the cost of scanning one region, $c_x$ the cost
of one cross-region active interaction, and $p$ the fraction of calls eligible
for the interaction measure. The expected cost, in common units, is
\[
C=q c_q R_{90}+p c_x X.
\]
Under this model, ALPS is cheaper than METIS when
$q c_q/c_x>p(X_{\mathrm{ALPS}}-X_{\mathrm{METIS}})/
(R_{90}^{\mathrm{METIS}}-R_{90}^{\mathrm{ALPS}})$. Using the measured
$p=0.457$--$0.606$, the threshold is $0.020$--$0.081$ across
Table~\ref{tab:headtohead}. The corresponding threshold against TxAllo is
$0.011$--$0.119$. These thresholds cover operating costs before allocator
computation. A deployment with a different query mix should replace $R_{90}$
with the weighted mean for that mix. The model therefore makes clear when
retrieval locality may justify a higher interaction cut; it does not claim
that ALPS is always better.

The model can also include the cost of rebuilding an allocation. Let $A_m$
be the cost of one rebuild by allocator $m$, measured in the same units as
$c_q$ and $c_x$. Let $N$ be the number of submitted transactions and
$Q=qN$ the number of token queries before the next rebuild. Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:amortised_cost}
\bar C_m=A_m/N+q c_q R_m+p c_x X_m.
\end{equation}
Let $\Delta A=A_{\rm ALPS}-A_B$ and
$\Delta X=X_{\rm ALPS}-X_B$. ALPS is cheaper than baseline $B$ only when
$Q c_q(R_B-R_{\rm ALPS})>\Delta A+Np c_x\Delta X$.
When $R_B>R_{\rm ALPS}$, the rebuild cost adds
$\Delta A/[N c_x(R_B-R_{\rm ALPS})]$ to the earlier threshold. More frequent
rebuilding increases this cost. If the rebuild interval is $\tau$ and the
transaction rate is $v$, then $N=v\tau$. This model assumes that query and
interaction statistics remain stable during that interval; we do not test
that assumption.

In the five-seed, fresh-process, full-trace allocator measurements reported in
Section~\ref{sec:components}, ALPS takes 10.87--15.45~s more than METIS to
allocate the regions. If we assume an equal-recall query saving of
$1~\mu$s, 10.87--15.45 million queries would be needed to recover this extra
computation alone. This is an illustrative calculation, not a deployment
measurement. A full comparison must also include shared resource costs,
interaction costs, migration, and index rebuilding. Complete queries scan all
regions and rows, so these experiments do not show an allocation-related scan
saving for complete queries.

Table~\ref{tab:retrieval} reports the query scan results. Oracle-90 gives
ALPS 96.2\% recall, so it is an approximate result, not a complete query.
Under History-90, ALPS reaches 91.4\% recall while scanning 2.74 regions and
4,873 rows. Compared with METIS and TxAllo, it scans 38.8--39.7\% fewer
regions and 41.8\% fewer rows. Its recall is also 2.9--4.1 percentage points
higher. S-KM-BAL contacts 8.6\% fewer regions than ALPS, but ALPS scans
15.1\% fewer rows and has 7.8 percentage points higher recall.

A complete query scans all six regions and returns every match. Its mean
per-token median latency is 7.93--8.87~$\mu$s. History-90 reports the median
and interquartile range over 250 in-memory repetitions; these timings establish
neither distributed nor equal-recall latency gains.

The matched-90\% results also allow an example of rebuild amortisation
against METIS$+$C. ALPS contacts 1.77 fewer regions, but its conditional cut
is .495 higher at coverage $p=.348$. In the 70/30 prospective experiment
underlying Table~\ref{tab:prospective}, the timed operation is the history-prefix
static allocator alone; future routing and query execution are excluded.
Averaged over four traces and three seeds, it takes 9.36~s for ALPS and
0.124~s for METIS$+$C, a 9.24~s difference. Under the cost model
in~\eqref{eq:amortised_cost}, assume $q=.10$
queries per transaction, a regional query cost of 1~ms, and an active-
interaction cost of .1~ms. The estimated break-even point is about
$5.8\times10^4$ transactions. If the interaction cost is 1~ms, it rises to
$1.87\times10^6$ transactions. If $q=.01$ and the interaction cost remains
.1~ms, it rises to $1.87\times10^7$ transactions. These are example
scenarios, not measured service costs. If query savings per transaction do
not exceed the interaction penalty, the extra allocation cost is not
recovered by this model.

The prospective sensitivity test varies
$\lambda_g\in\{.15,.60,2.40\}$ in both history fitting and causal placement.
The protocol and query targets stay fixed across four traces and three seeds;
no parameter is selected using suffix results. At the 90\% target, the mean
cuts are .592, .434, and .179. The corresponding recalls are 90.78\%,
91.45\%, and 89.26\%; scanned rows are 4,404, 5,262, and 6,564; and loads
are 1.534, 1.614, and 1.551. The 0.67 percentage-point increase in mean
recall at $\lambda_g=.60$ is descriptive and is not a significance claim.

The artifact reports variation across seeds within each trace separately from
variation across the four different traces. The pooled standard deviation
across 12 configurations is only a descriptive summary. A clean-environment
rerun reproduced the scientific fields in Tables II--VI and Figures 2--4.

this check. A higher graph weight reduces interaction cut but increases scan
work and does not reliably improve load.

For receipt validation, we check a uniform sample of 100 C1 calls from seed
0. The sample confirms successful \texttt{Transfer} logs, including in offline
replay. Ethereum receipt validation remains unresolved. The supplementary
artifact records the attempts and their classifications. A missing receipt
does not prove that a transaction reverted. This sample does not validate the
Ethereum workload or reconstruct historical balances.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
Guarded-key locality exactly characterises balance-safe partitions in the
uncoordinated same-snapshot additive-merge model; sender locality additionally
keeps nonce dependencies local for the evaluated direct calls. ALPS uses these
constraints to combine semantic, interaction, and load objectives, meeting its
static load target in every reported run.

Empirically, ALPS improves retrieval scan work but trades interaction locality
and prospective load against graph-oriented methods. It is therefore suitable
when selective-query savings outweigh cut, imbalance, and rebuild costs; the
results establish neither universal superiority nor production-speed gains.
Chronological placement provides no lifetime load guarantee.

Generality is limited by short direct-call traces, unresolved Ethereum receipt
validation, absent historical-balance reconstruction, in-memory queries, and an
allocation-only TxAllo comparison. Future work will study receipt-aware archive
replay, general read/write sets, indexed queries, migration, and protocol
integration.
